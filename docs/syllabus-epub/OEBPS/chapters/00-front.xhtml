Cross Creek 101

A Hands-On Training Syllabus for the Cross Creek ICS/OT Security Range

By Britley Hoff

2026 Edition

In November 2023 a water authority in Aliquippa, Pennsylvania lost control of a booster station. Not to a zero-day, not to a nation-state exploit chain. To a programmable logic controller sitting on the public internet with the password still set to the number one, four times. A group calling itself CyberAv3ngers walked in the front door and put a message on the screen, and they did the same thing to water systems in several other states. A few months later, in Lviv, malware called FrostyGoop spoke plain Modbus to a rack of heating controllers and left six hundred apartment buildings cold for two days in January.

None of that was clever. That is the part people miss. The controllers that run water plants and substations were designed in an era when "on the network" meant "on a serial cable in a locked room," and most of them still trust anything that can reach the port. The attacks that are working right now are the boring ones: default credentials, a protocol with no authentication, a flat network, an engineering laptop nobody segmented off.

I have spent a good part of my career on the other side of this. Alongside the mainframe work I am a Master-level industrial electrician, and I have wired, programmed, and troubleshot Siemens and Allen-Bradley PLCs and their ladder logic on the kind of equipment this course simulates. I have stood in the MCC room. I know what a metering pump sounds like when it is running wide open, and I know that "just restore from the golden program" is a sentence that assumes somebody kept a golden program.

This is a seven-session course built entirely on the Cross Creek range: a mock municipal water treatment plant and a small power substation, driven by three simulated controllers that speak the real protocols, Modbus/TCP, EtherNet/IP, and Siemens S7comm. A Python process simulator plays the physical world, coarsely and on purpose. Open a valve and the chlorine rises. Stop a pump and the tank drains. Every command in this book was run against the actual containers before it went to print.

The first four sessions are the attack half. You will find an internet-exposed HMI and log in with the factory account, stop the distribution pump with twenty lines of Python, drive the chlorine residual past four parts per million, halt a substation RTU, and replace a running control program with one that has the high-level interlock cut out. The last three are the defense half, run against the same range with one flag flipped: you segment the network, put a source allowlist on the controllers, lock the keyswitches, stand up an ICS-aware sensor, and rehearse the recovery. Then a capstone that chains the whole attack and then closes every hole, followed by an incident-response tabletop.

A training environment that is honest about its own edges is more useful than one that hopes you will not notice them. Where Cross Creek uses a stand-in, this book says so. The Allen-Bradley logic download, for instance, is not the real Studio 5000 wire format, because that cannot be reproduced without Rockwell's tooling; it is an unauthenticated service that swaps the controller's logic, and it teaches the concept and the detection, not the packet layout. That is called out where it happens.

Standing Up the Range

Cross Creek is one docker-compose stack. Ten containers on five bridge networks laid out along the Purdue model: an edge network for the attacker, an IT network, a DMZ, one flat OT network for the HMIs and PLCs, and an internal field bus. Every published port binds to 127.0.0.1. The attacker box has no route to the internet, and status.sh proves it.

cd crosscreek
./setup.sh              # preflight, build the images
./start.sh              # bring it up with the deliberate weaknesses on
./status.sh             # health, the loopback bind audit, the containment probe
./start.sh --segmented  # the defended topology, for Sessions 5 to 7
./reset.sh              # restore the golden state between cohorts

Authorised Use

Use Cross Creek only: on your own machine; in a lab you control; in a classroom or range where you have permission; under written rules of engagement for authorised training or assessment. Never point a Cross Creek command, script, payload, scan, or credential at a real utility, a real PLC, or any system you do not own. ICS attacks have physical consequences. People can be hurt. This range exists so you never have to learn that on a live one.
