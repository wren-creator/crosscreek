About the Author

Britley Hoff is a Master Electrician, combat veteran, and systems architect who has spent twenty-five years with her hands on keyboards and in control cabinets rather than in a classroom. She holds a GED, no college degree, and a stack of certifications and shipped systems instead. Her mainframe work covers RACF, z/OS, TN3270, TSO, and z/VM, ten years of it hands-on, currently as Technology Owner for zLinux at a large health insurer. Her industrial work covers Siemens and Allen-Bradley PLCs, ladder logic, motor control, and the physical plant that ICS security spends most of its time abstracting away from.

She writes training that assumes you learn a system by standing one up and breaking it, not by reading about it. The Mainframe 100 and 200 series, the Widgetorium web pen-testing lab, and now Cross Creek all follow the same shape: a self-contained environment you run on your own machine, a set of deliberate weaknesses documented down to the command, and a syllabus that works the manual way first so you can tell the difference between a clean scan and a blind spot.

The outsider's view is the point, not an apology for it. No formal computer-science background means no inherited assumptions about how it has always been done, which turns out to be an advantage when the field you are looking at was built on assumptions that stopped being true twenty years ago.

A Closing Note

Nobody gets to practice on a real water plant before they need to already know one. There is no staging environment for a booster station. The operator who first learns what an unauthenticated Modbus write does is learning it from an incident, at two in the morning, with a low-pressure alarm going and a manager on the phone. That is a bad classroom.

Cross Creek is the classroom instead. It is deliberately small. The physics is arithmetic. The Allen-Bradley download is a stand-in, and the book says so. But the protocols are real, the attacks are the ones working right now, and the defenses are the ones the after-action reports keep recommending and plants keep not doing. If you can run the capstone chain, write it up, and then break every link of it in the segmented range, you understand the problem better than most of the people currently responsible for it.

The next gap is yours to build. Wastewater. Gas pressure regulation. DNP3 and IEC 61850 on the substation side. A malware-analysis module with a FrostyGoop-style sample in a sandbox. A safety-instrumented-system tier and a Triton-style scenario. The roadmap in the repo lists them. Pick one, build it the way this one is built, honest about its edges, and hand it to the next person who needs to already know.
